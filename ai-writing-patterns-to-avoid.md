# AI Writing Patterns to Avoid

A checklist for catching "AI voice" before publishing. Most of these are defaults to break, not hard bans. One em-dash, one rhetorical question, one triad is fine. What reads as machine-written is the _repetition_ and the _predictable rhythm_, the sense that every sentence was built from the same three molds. Examples marked ✗ / ✓ are pulled from real edits on our own drafts.

---

## The 60-second pre-publish check

Run these before you hit publish:

1. Search the file for `—` and `–`. Replace connector dashes with a comma, colon, period, or parentheses.
2. Search for the crutch words in section 6. If a word appears 3+ times, vary it.
3. Read the first sentence of every paragraph out loud. If they all have the same shape, break the pattern.
4. Find every "not X, but Y" / "it's not X, it's Y" and cut most of them.
5. Check the last line of each section. If it's a tidy summary or a lone "profound" one-liner, delete or fold it in.
6. Search for trailing tags: `honestly`, `basically`, `exactly`, `I guess`, `I know`. Keep one or two in the whole piece, cut the rest.
7. Scan for runs of **bolded** stats or phrases. If more than a couple, unbold most of them.
8. Ask: would a person actually say this sentence, or is it "writing"?

## 1. Em-dashes as connectors

The single most recognizable tell. AI reaches for the em-dash to glue clauses and drop in asides.

- ✗ "with more power come more security nightmares — and it only gets worse"
- ✓ "with more power come more security nightmares." (new sentence), or use a comma / colon / parentheses

Same goes for en-dashes used as connectors. Keep dashes only where they are genuinely the right mark (and even then, sparingly). Ranges like dates are fine, though we chose to convert even those to colons in our house style.

---

## 2. The antithesis reflex ("not X, but Y")

AI loves setting up a fake contrast to sound insightful. It comes in several disguises:

- "it's not about speed, it's about trust"
- "X, not Y" ("measured, not theoretical")
- "not just X, but Y"
- "enabling, not blocking"

These feel punchy in isolation and exhausting in bulk. Say the real thing directly.

- ✗ "that is not a productivity result. that is a story about feeling productive."
- ✓ "the gap is the real finding. it's easy to feel productive while cleaning up after an intern."

---

## 3. Mirror / echo constructions

Two halves of a sentence built to rhyme structurally (chiasmus), or "A is the new B" formulas. Reads clever, scans as generated.

- ✗ "the thing we most need to guard is the thing we just put in charge"
- ✗ "nobody was minding the gate, because we hadn't built the gate yet"
- ✓ break the symmetry, or just make the point once

---

## 4. Punch fragments for drama

The staccato "statement. fragment. fragment." used to sound profound.

- ✗ "that's what 2026 is about. not smarter agents. the locks for them."
- ✓ "that's the story of 2026. we built capable agents and never built the part that can tell one of them no."

One-sentence paragraphs are good for rhythm. Sentence _fragments_ strung together for effect are the tell.

---

## 5. The rule of three (and anaphora)

- **Triads as filler**: three adjectives or three clauses where one or two would do. A genuine list of three real things is fine. A rhythmic "fast, cheap, and reliable" flourish is not.
- **Anaphora**: repeated sentence openings for effect.
  - ✗ "it is the year we secure agents. and it is the year security goes agentic."
  - ✗ "engineers smell it. regulators smell it too. attackers smelled it first."
  - ✓ vary the structure: "engineers feel it, and it's made them careful. the regulators are asking sharper questions. the attackers noticed first and got busy."

---

## 6. Crutch words (search and destroy)

Words AI overuses to sound thoughtful. Not banned, but if one shows up repeatedly, it's a flag. Ctrl-F each before publishing:

`quietly` · `subtly` · `seamlessly` · `effortlessly` · `delve` · `dive into` / `deep dive` · `unpack` · `underscore` · `highlight` · `leverage` · `harness` · `robust` · `testament` · `landscape` · `realm` · `tapestry` · `navigate` (figurative) · `foster` · `pivotal` · `crucial` · `vital` · `myriad` · `plethora` · `boasts` · `notably` · `arguably` · `ever-evolving` · `game-changer` · `paradigm` · `holistic` · `nuanced` · `intricate` · `meticulous`

(We hit `quietly` seven times in one draft. That's the pattern to catch.)

---

## 7. Opener and transition clichés

- "In today's world / In the age of AI / In an era where…"
- "Enter [thing]." as a reveal
- "Here's the thing:" / "Here's the kicker:" / "But here's the catch:"
- "Let's dive in" / "Buckle up" / "Grab a coffee"
- "Make no mistake" / "Let that sink in"
- "It's worth noting that" / "It's important to remember"
- "A field note on…" / cutesy subtitle framings
- Rhetorical-question openers used as a crutch on every section

---

## 8. Hype and empty intensifiers

Vague scale-words that assert importance instead of showing it.

`revolutionary` · `transformative` · `cutting-edge` · `state-of-the-art` · `next-level` · `supercharge` · `unlock` · `elevate` · `empower` · `unleash` · `redefine` · `seismic shift` · `at the forefront` · `the future of X`

Show the thing. Let the reader decide it's a big deal.

---

## 9. Over-hedging and false balance

- "It's important to note that…", "That said,", "It's worth mentioning"
- Reflexive both-sides-ing: "while X has benefits, it also has drawbacks" on every claim
- Qualifying every sentence into mush ("somewhat", "arguably", "in many cases", "to some extent")

Have an opinion. Hedge only where the evidence actually forces it.

---

## 10. Structural and formatting tells

- **Uniform paragraphs**: every one the same 3-4 sentences. Vary length hard, mix one-liners with longer runs.
- **Bold-lead bullet lists everywhere**: the "**Term** — one sentence" pattern repeated for the whole piece. Fine occasionally, exhausting as the default.
- **The tidy summary close**: ending every section (and the piece) with a neat restatement. "In conclusion", "In summary", "Ultimately".
- **Title Case Headings** when the voice is casual (our house style is lowercase headings).
- **Emoji sprinkling** to fake warmth.
- **Perfect parallelism** in every list and heading set.
- **The recap/TL;DR section**: closing the piece with a "let's sum up / key takeaways" block that restates points already made in bold. Fine for reference docs; in an essay it's belt-and-suspenders and reads generated.

---

## 11. Trailing conversational tags

AI fakes a casual voice by tacking a filler word onto the end of a sentence: `honestly`, `basically`, `exactly`, `really`, `I guess`, `I know`, `or something`. One or two across a whole piece is human. On every other paragraph it's a costume.

- ✗ "Saved by a bug in the malware, basically." … "The PocketOS god-mode token, exactly." … "Terrible conference talk, I know."
- ✓ keep at most one or two; let the other sentences stand without the tag

---

## 12. The section-ending aphorism (the "drop")

Distinct from the tidy summary (§10): a short, standalone, emotionally-weighted line dropped at the end of a section to sound profound. Build-up, then a mic-drop one-liner on its own paragraph.

- ✗ "The agents are already inside the building."
- ✗ "We simply never built the thing that checks."
- ✗ "That's how a checkbox turns into a category."
- ✓ fold the point into the paragraph above, or end on a concrete detail instead of an abstraction. Earn the closer, don't manufacture it.

The last line of the _whole piece_ is allowed to land. Every section landing this way is the tell.

---

## 13. Escalation scaffolding

Phrases that announce the next sentence is important instead of just making it important: "here's the uncomfortable part", "and it gets worse", "the brutal one is", "the sharpest cut is", "and systemically it's worse", "make no mistake". Also the "this isn't just X, it's Y" escalation and "and here's the part that's easy to miss".

- ✗ "And here's the uncomfortable part, piling pressure onto an agent doesn't make it careful."
- ✓ "Piling pressure onto an agent doesn't make it careful." Just say it; let the sentence carry its own weight.

---

## 14. Self-posed question then instant answer

Setting up a question only to answer it in the same breath, as a rhythm device: "Why does X matter? Mostly because…", "So what can go wrong? Turns out, a lot.", "So if these are architecture problems, what's the architecture?". One earns its place. As the default way to open or bridge every section, it's a tell (related to the rhetorical-question crutch in §7).

- ✗ "Why does an OWASP list matter? Mostly because it becomes the shared language…"
- ✓ "An OWASP list matters because it becomes the shared language…"

---

## 15. Bolding for drama

Bolding stats or phrases to manufacture emphasis, especially a run of bolded numbers stacked in one paragraph. Reads like a pitch deck, not prose.

- ✗ "**90% over-permissioned** … **88% of orgs** … **45 to 1** … **150,000+ agents**"
- ✓ bold sparingly, if at all. A wall of bold trains the reader to skim. Let the numbers carry themselves in plain text.

---

## 16. Transformation and "X did it too" framing

- **"From X to Y"** as a stock arc: "from chaotic to more chaotic", "from a checkbox to a category". Occasionally apt, tiresome as a reflex.
- **Escalating enumeration** where each example one-ups the last for rhythm ("A did the thing. B did too. C did it first."). Real examples are fine; the _ladder_ built for drama is the tell (overlaps with anaphora, §5).
- **Listicle framings** to introduce a run: "Quick tour", "Quick reel", "A quick recap". Signposting the format instead of just writing it.

---

## What's actually fine (don't overcorrect)

The goal is to sound like a person, not to ban rhetoric. In moderation, all of these are human:

- one em-dash in a whole piece, where it's the right mark
- a real list of three
- a rhetorical question that earns its place
- loose grammar and comma splices that match a casual voice (not so many they read as errors)
- starting a sentence with "and", "but", "so"

The problem is never one instance. It's the same move on repeat.

---

## Our house style (the positive version)

- lowercase, conversational, opinionated
- vary sentence length on purpose; let some run loose
- concrete over abstract: a real story or number beats an adjective
- cite real sources, flag single-sourced claims
- a little imperfect grammar is welcome, abuse is not
- kill filler; if a sentence doesn't earn its place, cut it
- when in doubt, say it the way you'd say it to a smart friend, then tidy lightly
